East or West?

A Deep-Learning Dialect Classifier for Ancient Aramaic Texts. It locates the Dead Sea Scrolls, Enoch, Targum Onkelos and the Aramaic Levi Document on the Bavli (Eastern) to Yerushalmi (Western) axis.
Oria Drori & Yahel Omesi  · Advisor: Prof. Lee-Ad Gottlieb
Department of Computer Science, Ariel University
Yerushalmi  (Western / Jerusalem)
Bavli  (Eastern / Babylonian)
1 · Research Objective & Motivation
Motivation

Pinning down the exact dialect of ancient texts such as the Dead Sea Scrolls is a hard, long-debated problem. Scribes wrote in a formal, standardized "literary" Aramaic that masks the regional spoken language underneath.

Goal

Build a deep-learning model that looks past surface vocabulary and spelling and reads the deep structural and syntactic patterns of a text: the fingerprints a scribe leaves without noticing.

Approach

Train a sequence model on the two poles of Late Aramaic, Eastern (Bavli) and Western (Yerushalmi), then measure the dialectal affinity of unlabeled ancient corpora.

"Can a neural network hear the accent a scribe tried to hide?"
2 · Methodology

To keep the model honest, we feed it no raw text, only 8 linguistic hypotheses, operationalized as 11 numerical features about structure and grammar:

1Noun state: emphatic vs. absoluteemphatic_ratio · absolute_ratio
2Function-word densityfunction_words_ratio
3Lexical diversity (Type-Token Ratio)lexical_diversity
4Syntactic distribution (verb load)verb_ratio
5Voice: active vs. passivepassive_voice_ratio
6Pluralityplural_ratio
7Sentence / line lengthline_length · avg_word_len
8Syntactic transitions after a verbv_then_noun_ratio · v_then_prep_ratio

What the tree shows. We ran this clustering without giving the model any labels, and it still split the Bavli tractates from the Yerushalmi tractates into two clean groups on its own (28 and 28, with no mixing). For us that was the real check: it means our 11 features are picking up the actual dialect difference and not some accident of the data. The texts we were testing also grouped together in sensible ways, with the Onkelos books forming their own cluster and the narrative texts (Enoch, the scrolls, Aramaic Levi) forming another. The tree does not place these texts inside the Yerushalmi branch, so we are not claiming that directly. The Western lean is the separate pole-distance result, where each text ends up closer to the Yerushalmi centroid, and that result still held after we dropped the two line-length features.
4 · Conclusions & Discussion

Across four independent corpora, the model consistently places the texts on the Western (Yerushalmi) side of the axis, on the strength of structure alone.

What it does (and doesn't) claim. The classifier learns Late Aramaic (the two Talmuds), while the target texts are older Middle Aramaic. So the claim is not that these texts are the Jerusalem Talmud. It measures which later dialectal pole their grammar most resembles, and the answer is emphatically the Western branch.

Why it's interesting. The formal "literary" register was expected to look Eastern. Instead the spoken Western substrate leaks through into grammar and word order. Targum Onkelos is a striking case: it was transmitted through Babylonia, yet its structure comes out Western.

Robustness. The Western lean holds three ways: the supervised classifier, the unsupervised clustering, and a version with the two line-length features removed.
